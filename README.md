## Reflection

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I interpret user needs by first identifying the problem the user wants to solve, the outcome they expect, and the conditions that will show the feature is successful. I then translate those needs into clear, testable requirements before designing or coding the solution. User stories help by describing a feature from the user's perspective and connecting it to a specific benefit. Their acceptance criteria also give developers and testers a shared definition of completion. In the SNHU Travel project, this approach kept the work focused on what a traveler needed to see and do rather than on technical features alone.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I approach program development incrementally: I clarify requirements, divide the work into manageable tasks, build a small working portion, test it, and use feedback to decide what to improve next. This reduces the risk of discovering major misunderstandings late in development. In future projects, I plan to use prioritized product backlogs, sprint planning, daily check-ins, sprint reviews, and retrospectives. I also want to define acceptance criteria early and test continuously so that each increment remains usable and changes can be incorporated without destabilizing the entire product.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

A good software development team member communicates clearly, completes commitments responsibly, and raises blockers early enough for the team to respond. Good teamwork also requires listening to different perspectives, giving respectful and specific feedback, documenting important decisions, and helping the group pursue the sprint goal instead of focusing only on individual assignments. Because software development is collaborative, transparency and adaptability are as important as technical skill. A strong team member supports shared ownership of quality and treats reviews and retrospectives as opportunities to improve rather than assign blame.
